The last light in the bindery

By October the windows of the bindery on Rua da Madalena hold the afternoon a minute longer than the street. Marta keeps the kettle on the radiator and the radio on a station that still reads the shipping news. The issue on the bench is forty-one: a stack of sixteen-page signatures waiting for thread, a spine not yet a spine.

She says the work is mostly listening. Paper tells you when it will take glue and when it will cockle. A poem tells you whether it wants a wide margin or a tight one. The guest editors this season asked for more air around the short lyrics, so the trim is a centimetre taller than forty and the type sits a hair smaller. Nobody will measure it. Everybody will feel it.

The subscribers in Porto still get their copies by hand. A boy on a bicycle takes a canvas satchel down to the river and leaves the wrapped issues under the café awning before the first espresso is pulled. That route has not changed since issue eight, only the boy has.

Type was set in Lisbon, printed in Alcântara, and sewn in a room that still smells of hide glue. The errata card, this time, is a poem. It sits in the back, unnumbered, because nobody wanted to give a correction the dignity of a page.

Outside, the tram still complains on the hill. Inside, the last gatherer clips a waste sheet, blows the dust off the brass, and starts the sewing. Forty-one will leave the building on a Thursday, wrapped in kraft, addressed in a hand that has not changed since issue twelve.
